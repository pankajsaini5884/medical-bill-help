What to do when a medical bill goes to collections

Updated November 4, 2026 · Facts checked against the official sources listed at the end.

A letter or call from a debt collector about a medical bill is stressful, but it is not the end of the road. You have rights, and there are often ways to reduce or remove the debt, especially if the bill was wrong or you qualified for help you never got. Here is a calm, step-by-step plan.

Step 1: Don't ignore it, and don't pay on the spot

Ignoring a collector won't make a debt go away, and paying immediately can mean paying a bill that is wrong, already covered, or eligible for a discount. Start by getting the facts in writing. Write down the date of every contact, the company name, the person you spoke with, and what they said.

Step 2: Read (or ask for) the validation notice

Under the CFPB's debt collection rule, a debt collector generally must give you “validation information” about the debt, either in its first communication or within five days after. The CFPB lists what it generally must include:

  • a statement that the communication is from a debt collector;
  • your name and mailing information, and the collector's;
  • the name of the creditor you owe the debt to;
  • the account number, if any;
  • an itemization of the current amount reflecting interest, fees, payments and credits since a particular date;
  • the current amount of the debt;
  • information you can use to reply if you think the debt isn't yours or the amount is wrong; and
  • an end date for a 30-day period when you can dispute the debt.

If you didn't get this information, the CFPB says you can submit a complaint.

Step 3: Dispute within 30 days if anything is off

The CFPB says that once you receive the validation information, you have 30 days to dispute the debt in writing. If you send a written dispute or a request for information about the original creditor within that period, the collector must pause collecting the disputed amount until it has adequately responded. Missing the window or disputing only by phone can affect your ability to use these rights, so put it in writing and keep a copy.

Reasons to dispute include: the debt isn't yours, the amount is wrong, insurance should have paid it, you already paid it, or the bill contains errors.

Step 4: Go back to the original bill

Many medical debts in collections started as billing problems. Contact the original provider and:

  • Ask for an itemized bill and check it against your Explanation of Benefits and medical records. See how to find errors on a hospital bill.
  • Check whether insurance was billed correctly. A claim sent to the wrong insurer or with a wrong member ID can leave you with a balance you don't owe.
  • Check whether the claim was denied. If so, you may still be able to appeal. See how to appeal a denied claim, and watch the appeal deadline in the denial notice.
  • Check for surprise billing. If the bill is for out-of-network charges from an emergency visit or from care at an in-network hospital, it may be covered by the No Surprises Act.

Step 5: Apply for hospital financial assistance, even now

If the debt is from a nonprofit hospital, this may be your strongest option. IRS rules under Section 501(r)(6) require these hospitals to make reasonable efforts to find out whether you qualify for financial assistance before taking “extraordinary collection actions,” which include selling your debt, reporting it to credit bureaus, and legal actions such as lawsuits, liens, and wage garnishment. Hospitals are responsible for the actions of collection agencies and debt buyers they refer or sell debts to.

Key points from the IRS rules:

  • You generally have 240 days from the first bill after discharge to apply, and the hospital may continue to accept and process applications at any time.
  • A complete application suspends extraordinary collection actions while the hospital decides.
  • If you qualify, the hospital must refund overpayments (unless less than $5) and take reasonably available steps to reverse collection actions, including vacating judgments, lifting liens, and removing adverse information it reported to credit bureaus.

CMS recommends telling any debt collector that you're applying for financial assistance and asking them to pause collections while that process plays out. See our full guide to hospital charity care, or use the free letter tool to start your application.

Step 6: If the debt is valid, negotiate

If the bill is correct and you don't qualify for assistance, you still have options. CMS notes that hospitals and medical offices can sometimes lower the price or offer payment plans. If you reach an agreement with a collector, get it in writing before you pay, and keep proof of every payment.

Be careful with debt settlement companies. The CFPB warns that dealing with companies that promise to renegotiate or settle your debts can be risky.

Know the limits on collectors

  • The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act prohibits debt collectors from using abusive, unfair or deceptive practices (CFPB).
  • A collector generally needs a court judgment to garnish your wages or bank account, and it violates the FDCPA for a collector to threaten garnishment if your wages can't legally be garnished (CFPB).
  • Statutes of limitations limit how long a collector can sue over a debt. The CFPB says most fall in the three to six year range, but they vary by state and type of debt.
  • If you're sued, respond. The CFPB warns that if you don't respond to a legal complaint you lose your chance to defend yourself, and suggests talking to an attorney or a legal aid office.

Check your credit report

If a medical debt shows up on your credit report and you believe it's wrong, CMS points to the CFPB's guidance on disputing errors on your credit report. If you were approved for hospital financial assistance, the IRS rules require the hospital to take steps to remove adverse information it reported. Rules on how medical debt appears on credit reports have changed in recent years, so check the CFPB's current guidance for your situation.

Checklist

  • Log every contact; don't pay before you have the facts.
  • Get the validation notice and note the 30-day dispute deadline.
  • Dispute in writing if anything is wrong; keep a copy.
  • Get the itemized bill and check insurance processing.
  • Apply for hospital financial assistance and tell the collector you have applied.
  • If the debt is valid, negotiate and get any agreement in writing.
  • If you're sued, respond and seek legal help.
