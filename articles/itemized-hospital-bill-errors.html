How to ask for an itemized hospital bill and find errors

Updated November 4, 2026 · Facts checked against the official sources listed at the end.

The first bill most hospitals send is a summary: a few lines and a total. You cannot check a total. Before you pay a large medical bill, ask for the itemized version and go through it line by line. Here is how.

Summary bill vs. itemized bill

A summary statement groups charges into broad categories such as “pharmacy,” “lab,” or “supplies.” An itemized bill lists each item or service separately, usually with the date, a description, a billing code, the quantity, and the charge. CMS's patient guidance on checking a bill for errors starts with exactly this step: ask your provider's billing department for a detailed bill that lists the cost of each medical item or service you are being billed for.

Step 1: Request the itemized bill in writing

You can call the billing number on the statement, but a short letter or patient-portal message creates a record. Ask for:

  • Every charge, with the date of service and a plain-language description.
  • The billing code for each line (CPT, HCPCS, or revenue code) and the quantity.
  • All payments, insurance adjustments and discounts applied to the account.
  • Whether insurance was billed, when, and which insurer.

It is reasonable to ask the billing office to hold the account and not send it to collections while you review. They may or may not agree, so note who you spoke to and what they said. Our free letter tool writes this request for you.

Step 2: Gather what you need to compare

CMS suggests checking the bill against two other documents:

  • Your Explanation of Benefits (EOB), if you used insurance. Your insurer sends this after it processes a claim. CMS advises making sure the “your share” amount on the EOB matches what the bill says you owe. If the bill asks for more than the EOB says is your responsibility, ask the provider why before you pay.
  • Your medical records. CMS's rule of thumb: you should not get a bill for anything that isn't documented in your records. Some providers charge a fee for records, so ask about it first.

If you were uninsured or chose not to use insurance, also find your good faith estimate. Under the No Surprises Act, providers usually must give self-pay patients an estimate when care is scheduled at least 3 business days ahead or when the patient asks. If a provider's bill is at least $400 more than its estimate, you may be able to dispute it through a federal process (see below).

Step 3: Go through the bill line by line

Use a highlighter and look for these common problems:

Duplicate charges

The same service, test, or supply listed twice on the same date. CMS notes that double billing is especially common when you got care from more than one provider, so compare the hospital bill against any separate bills from doctors, labs, or imaging groups.

Services you didn't receive

A test that was ordered and then cancelled, a medication you refused, or a procedure that did not happen. Check against your records and your own memory of the visit.

Wrong dates or quantities

Charges dated after you were discharged, an extra room day, or a quantity that looks too high (for example, a medication billed ten times when you took it twice).

Codes that don't match the care

CMS suggests searching each billing code with the words “medical billing code” and reading the description. If the description doesn't match what happened — for example, a code for a longer or more complex visit than you had — ask the billing department to explain or correct it.

Insurance problems

A claim that was never sent to your insurer, sent to the wrong insurer, or sent with your name or member ID wrong. These often explain a surprisingly large balance and can usually be fixed by resubmitting the claim.

Payments not credited

Make sure any copay or deposit you paid at the visit appears as a credit.

Step 4: Raise the errors with the billing department

Call or write to the billing office with a list of the specific lines you are questioning: the date, description, code and amount, and why you believe each is wrong. Ask them to correct the bill and send you a revised itemized statement. If your insurer is involved, CMS notes the insurance company may be able to fix billing errors directly with your provider, so contact them too.

Keep it factual and polite. Write down the date, the name of the person you spoke with, and what they agreed to do. If you send a letter, keep a copy.

Ask about the hospital's financial assistance policy at the same time. Nonprofit hospitals must have one, and even if your bill is correct, you may qualify for a discount or free care. See our guide to hospital charity care.

Step 5: Know where to escalate

  • Insurance denied or underpaid the claim? You can appeal the insurer's decision. See how to appeal a denied claim.
  • Surprise out-of-network charges for emergency care or for care at an in-network hospital may be prohibited by the No Surprises Act. See our No Surprises Act guide. You can call the No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059.
  • Self-pay and billed $400 or more over your good faith estimate? CMS's patient-provider dispute process requires that you start within 120 calendar days of the initial bill and pay a $25 non-refundable administrative fee. While the dispute is underway, the provider can't move the bill to collections.
  • Need a hand? CMS points patients to their state's Consumer Assistance Program and to patient advocates, who can help with billing issues.

Quick checklist

  • Request the itemized bill in writing; ask for a hold on collections while you review.
  • Get your EOB (if insured) or good faith estimate (if self-pay).
  • Request your medical records for the dates of service.
  • Check for duplicates, services not received, wrong dates, quantities and codes, and missing credits.
  • Send the billing office a written list of disputed lines and ask for a corrected bill.
  • Ask about financial assistance.
