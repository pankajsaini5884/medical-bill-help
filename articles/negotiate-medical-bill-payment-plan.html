How to negotiate a medical bill and set up a payment plan

Updated November 5, 2026 · Facts checked against the official sources listed at the end.

A large medical bill isn't always the final word. CMS, the federal agency behind the patient billing-rights pages, says providers and their billing offices can sometimes lower the price or offer payment plans. Here's how to ask well, without guessing.

First, make sure you're negotiating the right number

Negotiating a bill that contains mistakes means haggling over money you may not owe at all. CMS recommends checking your bill before you talk price: ask for a detailed (itemized) bill, compare it with your Explanation of Benefits (EOB) if you have insurance, and look for duplicate or unfamiliar charges. Our guide on how to ask for an itemized bill and find errors walks through it.

Also rule out two things that can wipe out a bill entirely or cut it sharply:

  • A surprise out-of-network bill. If it came from an emergency visit or an out-of-network doctor at an in-network hospital, the No Surprises Act may limit you to in-network cost-sharing.
  • Financial assistance (charity care). CMS notes that nonprofit hospitals must give financial assistance to eligible patients who can't afford to pay, and other providers may offer it too. See hospital charity care explained.

If you were uninsured or self-pay and received a good faith estimate, check whether the bill is at least $400 more than the estimate. If it is, CMS says you can use the patient-provider dispute resolution process; you have to start within 120 calendar days of the bill's date.

Step 1: Research a fair price

CMS's own advice for talking to a provider about a bill starts with price research:

  • Hospital price transparency. Hospitals must make their standard charges public. Search for “hospital price transparency [hospital name]” to find your hospital's file.
  • Call around. Call nearby hospitals or facilities and ask what they charge for each CPT (procedure) code on your itemized bill. Ask for the “usual, customary, and reasonable charge” for your area.

Write the numbers down next to each line of your bill. A specific comparison (“the hospital down the road lists this code at a much lower price”) gives the billing office something concrete to respond to.

Step 2: Decide what you're asking for

Know your ask before you dial. Common requests include:

  • a reduction of the balance, based on your price research or a self-pay rate;
  • a discount if you can pay a lump sum now (only offer this if you truly can);
  • a monthly payment plan at an amount you can sustain — ask whether it carries any interest or fees;
  • a hold on collections while you apply for financial assistance or while a billing question is reviewed.

Pick a monthly figure you can keep paying even in a tight month. A plan you can't keep can leave you worse off than asking for a lower payment up front.

Step 3: Call the billing office (a simple script)

CMS suggests calling your provider if you can't afford the bill and asking whether they can put you on a payment plan or reduce it, mentioning your price research. Here is a calm way to say that:

“Hi, I'm calling about account number [number] for [patient name], date of service [date]. I've reviewed the itemized bill and I want to resolve it, but I can't pay [amount] as billed. I looked up [hospital/area] prices for these codes and found [what you found]. Is there a self-pay or prompt-pay discount, or can you reduce the balance? If not, can we set up a payment plan of [amount] a month with no interest? I'd also like to know whether I qualify for your financial assistance policy.”

  • Ask for the name of the person you speak with and any reference number. CMS recommends noting these in case you need to follow up.
  • If the first person can't help, politely ask whether a supervisor or a financial counselor can review the account.
  • Don't agree to anything you can't afford just to end the call. It's fine to say, “Let me think about that and call back.”

Step 4: Get it in writing before you pay

Ask the billing office to send any reduction or payment plan in writing — the new balance, the monthly amount, the due date, and whether interest or fees apply. Then send a short confirmation letter of your own. Keep copies of everything, and keep proof of every payment.

A short follow-up letter can look like this:

[Date]
Re: Account [number], patient [name], date of service [date]

This confirms my phone call on [date] with [name] (reference [number]). We agreed that [the balance will be reduced to $___ / I will pay $___ per month starting ___, with no interest or fees]. Please send written confirmation of this agreement and an updated statement. Please do not send this account to collections while I make the agreed payments.

[Your name, address, phone]

If the bill is already in collections

The same approach works, with extra protections. You can ask the collector to validate the debt, and you have 30 days to dispute it in writing. If you're applying for financial assistance, CMS suggests telling debt collectors so and asking them to pause collections. See what to do when a medical bill goes to collections.

Free help if you're stuck

CMS points patients to patient advocates, who can handle billing issues on your behalf, and to the No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059 for questions about your rights. Your state's Consumer Assistance Program may also be able to help with insurance-related billing problems.

A simple checklist

  • Get the itemized bill and check it against your EOB.
  • Rule out a surprise-billing violation and check for financial assistance.
  • Look up the hospital's public prices and nearby prices for your CPT codes.
  • Decide your ask: lower balance, lump-sum discount, or an affordable payment plan.
  • Call, take names and reference numbers, and stay calm and specific.
  • Get every agreement in writing before you pay; keep proof of payment.
