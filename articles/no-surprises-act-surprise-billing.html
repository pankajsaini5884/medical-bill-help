The No Surprises Act, explained

Updated November 4, 2026 · Facts checked against the official sources listed at the end.

You went to an in-network hospital, did everything right, and then a bill arrived from a doctor you never chose who turned out to be out of network. Since 2022, a federal law protects most people with private insurance from many of these surprise bills. Here's what it covers and what to do if it's broken.

What the law is

The No Surprises Act is a federal law that took effect on January 1, 2022. According to CMS, it protects you from unexpected out-of-network bills for:

  • emergency room visits;
  • non-emergency care related to a visit to an in-network hospital, hospital outpatient department, or ambulatory surgical center; and
  • air ambulance services.

These protections apply if you have health insurance through an employer (including a Federal Employees Health Benefits plan), the federal Health Insurance Marketplace, a state-based Marketplace, or other individual market coverage. The law also gives people without insurance, or not using it, the right to a good faith estimate (more on that below).

Emergency care

When you go to an emergency room — including independent freestanding emergency departments — CMS says that, in most cases, if your plan covers emergency care you can't be charged more than your in-network cost-sharing (your copay, coinsurance or deductible) for emergency services. That applies to the hospital, the providers who treat you there, and an air ambulance provider if you were brought in by air.

Protections can extend to care after you are stabilized (“post-stabilization services”). In limited situations an out-of-network provider may ask you to sign a notice and consent form for that care, which waives the protections. CMS says they may only ask if you can travel to a nearby in-network provider without medical or emergency transportation and you are in a condition to give informed consent.

Planned care at an in-network facility

If your hospital or surgery center is in your network, you're protected from out-of-network charges by providers who work there but aren't in your network — for example, an out-of-network attending physician at your in-network hospital. These protections don't apply in other settings, such as a doctor's office that isn't a hospital outpatient department, or if you choose an out-of-network facility.

The notice-and-consent form: read before you sign

CMS warns that signing a notice and consent form means you agree to get care out of network and give up your protections. It is your choice, and you can decline, though the provider may then refuse to provide non-emergency care and you may need to reschedule with an in-network provider.

Some providers are not allowed to ask you to sign it at all. Per CMS, you shouldn't get a consent form:

  • for emergency services in the emergency room;
  • when no in-network provider is available at the facility for that service;
  • from assistant surgeons, hospitalists, or intensivists; or
  • for emergency medicine, anesthesiology, pathology, radiology, neonatology, or diagnostic services such as radiology and lab work.

When a form is allowed, it must be given separately from other paperwork, in the format you prefer (paper or email), available in any of the 15 most common languages in your state, and given in advance. If those rules weren't followed, CMS says to submit a complaint.

What the law does not cover

  • Ground ambulances. CMS states that ground ambulance services generally aren't covered by the No Surprises Act's billing protections and can still charge out-of-network rates, unless state law says otherwise.
  • Some plan types. Short-term limited duration plans, health care sharing ministries, and fixed indemnity plans (such as hospital indemnity insurance) aren't covered. The protections generally don't apply to vision-only and dental-only plans.
  • Medicare, Medicaid, Indian Health Services, VA health care and TRICARE already have their own protections against some out-of-network bills; CMS offers a separate action-plan tool for billing issues under those programs.
  • Your normal cost-sharing. CMS notes that getting a bill because you haven't met your deductible is not a violation.

When your doctor leaves your network

If you're a “continuing care patient” — for example, in treatment for a serious and complex illness, pregnant and in treatment, scheduled for non-elective surgery, receiving inpatient care, or terminally ill — CMS says you may be able to keep seeing that provider at in-network rates for up to 90 days after they leave your plan's network.

If you don't have or use insurance: good faith estimates

CMS explains that providers usually must give self-pay patients a good faith estimate when care is scheduled at least 3 business days ahead, or when you ask. Estimates cover one provider or facility at a time, so ask each provider involved. You won't get one during emergency care.

If a provider's bill is at least $400 more than its estimate, you may use the patient-provider dispute resolution process. You must start within 120 calendar days of the initial bill and pay a $25 non-refundable fee (if you win, the $25 is deducted from what you owe the provider). While the dispute is in progress, the provider can't send the bill to collections or threaten to, and must stop pursuing payment if it's already in collections.

What to do if you get a surprise bill

  1. Don't pay the out-of-network portion yet. Compare the bill with your Explanation of Benefits. Under the law, your cost should match in-network cost-sharing for protected care.
  2. Call the provider's billing office and say you believe the bill is covered by the No Surprises Act. Ask them to rebill at in-network cost-sharing. Write down who you spoke with.
  3. Call your insurer. If the insurer processed the claim as out of network, CMS says you can appeal the decision following your plan's process — see how to appeal a denied claim.
  4. Submit a complaint to the No Surprises Help Desk, online or at 1-800-985-3059, if a provider isn't following the rules. CMS says to do so if you received an out-of-network bill for an ER visit or a visit to an in-network facility, already paid more than in-network rates, or the notice-and-consent rules weren't followed.
  5. If a debt collector contacts you about an unexpected out-of-network bill, or it shows up on your credit report, CMS points you to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau at consumerfinance.gov or 1-855-411-2372.
