How to appeal a denied health insurance claim

Updated November 4, 2026 · Facts checked against the official sources listed at the end.

A denial letter is not the final word. Federal rules give most people with private health insurance the right to ask their plan to look again, and then to ask an independent reviewer if the plan still says no. Here is how that process works and how to put together an appeal that is hard to ignore.

First, know that you are not alone

Denials are routine. KFF's analysis of federal data found that insurers selling plans on HealthCare.gov denied 19% of in-network claims in 2024. Yet consumers appealed fewer than 1% of those denied claims, and when people did appeal, insurers upheld 66% of the denials at the internal appeal stage. Many people simply never ask. An appeal is not guaranteed to work, but you cannot win one you never file.

Step 1: Read the denial notice closely

Your plan has to tell you in writing why it denied the claim. HealthCare.gov lists the timelines for that notice: within 15 days for a prior-authorization request, within 30 days for a service you already received, and within 72 hours for urgent care. For job-based plans covered by ERISA, the Department of Labor says the notice must include the specific reason for the denial, the plan provision it relied on, a description of how to appeal, and — if the denial was about medical necessity or experimental treatment — an explanation of the clinical judgment or a statement that one will be provided free on request.

Look for three things:

  • The reason. “Not medically necessary,” “out of network,” “not a covered benefit,” “no prior authorization,” and coding or paperwork problems each call for a different response.
  • The deadline. Write it down the day the notice arrives.
  • Where and how to send the appeal. Some plans require their own form. HealthCare.gov notes you can also write a letter with your name, claim number and health insurance ID number.
Some denials are just paperwork. If the problem is a wrong code, a missing referral, or a typo in your name or ID, call your provider's billing office first. A corrected claim may get paid without a formal appeal. Keep notes of every call.

Step 2: Mind the deadlines

  • Filing an internal appeal: HealthCare.gov says you must file within 180 days (6 months) of receiving notice that your claim was denied. The Department of Labor says job-based plans must give you at least 180 days; your plan may allow longer.
  • The plan's decision: the internal appeal must be completed within 30 days if it is for a service you have not received yet, and within 60 days for a service you already received.
  • External review: you must file a written request within 4 months after you receive the final denial.

These are general federal timelines. Your plan's notice controls for your case, and some plans, such as Medicare and Medicaid, use different processes. If anything conflicts, follow your notice and ask the plan to confirm in writing.

Step 3: Gather your evidence

A strong appeal answers the specific reason for the denial with documents. Useful items include:

  • The denial notice and the Explanation of Benefits (EOB) showing what was not paid.
  • A letter from your doctor explaining why the treatment is needed for you, including what else has been tried.
  • Relevant medical records, test results, or notes.
  • The section of your plan documents that you believe covers the service.
  • The plan's own claim file. Under ERISA, your plan must give you copies of documents, records and other information relevant to your claim free of charge if you ask. Plans that are not grandfathered under the Affordable Care Act must also provide the diagnosis and treatment codes, and their meanings, on request.

HealthCare.gov also recommends keeping copies of everything you send and notes from every phone call: the date, time, name and title of the person you spoke with, and what they said. Send copies, keep the originals.

Step 4: Write the appeal letter

Keep it short, factual and organized. A good appeal letter:

  1. Identifies the patient, member ID, claim number, date of service and provider at the top.
  2. States clearly that you are requesting an internal appeal (a “full and fair review”) of the denial.
  3. Responds directly to the denial reason, in your own words, and points to the enclosed evidence.
  4. Asks for copies of the documents and criteria the plan used.
  5. Asks for a written decision.
  6. Lists the enclosures.

Avoid long histories, anger, or threats. The reviewer is looking for a reason to approve that fits the plan's rules. Under ERISA, the appeal must be reviewed by someone who was not involved in the original decision and who gives no deference to it, and the plan must consult a qualified medical professional when medical judgment is involved.

Send the appeal the way the notice tells you to. If you mail it, the Department of Labor suggests certified mail with return receipt so you have proof it arrived. Record the date sent.

If your situation is urgent

If waiting for a standard decision could seriously jeopardize your life or your ability to regain maximum function, you can ask for an expedited appeal. HealthCare.gov says that in urgent situations you may file an internal appeal and an external review request at the same time, and the final decision must come as quickly as your condition requires — no later than 4 business days after your request is received. Your doctor can help by stating that the case is urgent.

Step 5: If the plan still says no, ask for external review

External review sends your case to an independent third party. According to HealthCare.gov:

  • You must file a written request within 4 months after receiving the final denial.
  • Denials involving medical judgment, experimental or investigational treatment, and cancellation of coverage can go to external review.
  • Standard reviews must be decided no later than 45 days after the request is received; expedited reviews no later than 72 hours.
  • The insurer is required by law to accept the reviewer's decision.
  • If your plan uses the HHS-administered federal process, there is no charge; otherwise a fee may apply, but it cannot be more than $25 per external review.

Your final denial letter must tell you how to request external review. If it does not, ask the plan in writing.

Where to get free help

HealthCare.gov notes that your state's Consumer Assistance Program can file an appeal for you, and that it or your state Department of Insurance can help with external review. For job-based plans, the Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration has benefits advisors at 1-866-444-3272. A doctor's office that deals with appeals regularly can also be a strong ally.

A simple checklist

  • Write down the appeal deadline from your denial notice.
  • Call the provider to rule out a simple billing or coding error.
  • Request the plan's claim file and criteria.
  • Get a supporting letter from your doctor.
  • Send a clear appeal letter with enclosures; keep copies and proof of sending.
  • Log every call and letter. Follow up before the decision deadline.
  • If denied again, request external review within 4 months.
