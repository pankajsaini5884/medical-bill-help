Prior authorization denied? How to appeal

Updated November 5, 2026 · Facts checked against the official sources listed at the end.

Your doctor ordered a treatment, and your insurer said no before you could even get it. A prior authorization denial feels final, but it's a claim decision you can appeal — often faster than an ordinary claim. Here's how.

What a prior authorization denial is

Some plans require approval before you get a treatment, test, drug or procedure. The U.S. Department of Labor calls these pre-service claims: requests for approval required before medical care, such as preauthorization or a decision on whether a treatment is medically necessary. When the plan says no, that is a claim denial — and you have the same right to appeal it as any other denial.

The steps below describe the general federal rules for job-based (ERISA) plans and ACA-compliant individual plans. Medicare, Medicaid and some government or church plans use different processes, so always follow the instructions in your own denial notice.

How fast the plan must decide

For job-based plans, the Department of Labor's booklet sets these maximums for the first decision:

  • Urgent care: as soon as possible, and no more than 72 hours after the plan receives the claim. If a physician with knowledge of your condition tells the plan a pre-service claim is urgent, the plan must treat it as urgent.
  • Standard pre-service (prior authorization): within a reasonable time and no more than 15 days, with one possible 15-day extension if the plan notifies you in advance.

Step 1: Read the denial notice closely

Under the Department of Labor rules, the denial notice must give the specific reason, the plan provision relied on, a description of any extra information needed, and how to appeal. If the denial was based on medical necessity, the plan must explain the clinical judgment or tell you that explanation is free on request. Plans that aren't grandfathered under the ACA must also provide the diagnosis and treatment codes, and their meanings, if you ask.

Many denials turn out to be fixable paperwork problems: a missing form, a wrong code, or a request sent before required records arrived. Ask your doctor's office first whether they can correct and resubmit the request, or schedule a peer-to-peer conversation with the plan's reviewer if the plan offers one.

Step 2: Note the appeal deadlines

  • Filing: the Department of Labor says you have at least 180 days to appeal a denied claim under a job-based plan. HealthCare.gov likewise says to file an internal appeal within 180 days of the denial notice.
  • The plan's answer: for care you haven't received yet, HealthCare.gov says the internal appeal must be completed within 30 days. The Department of Labor's limit for a pre-service appeal is also 30 days (or 15 days per level if the plan has two levels).
  • Urgent appeals: the Department of Labor sets 72 hours for urgent-care appeals under job-based plans. HealthCare.gov says that in urgent situations you can file an internal appeal and an external review at the same time.

Step 3: Build the appeal around the denial reason

A prior authorization appeal usually wins or loses on medical necessity, so your doctor's input matters most. Useful items:

  • A letter of medical necessity from your doctor explaining why this treatment is needed for you, what else has been tried, and what's likely to happen without it.
  • Relevant records, test results and notes.
  • The plan's own coverage criteria for the service. You can request the documents, records and other information relevant to your claim free of charge.
  • The section of your plan documents that you believe covers the service.

Your appeal letter should name the patient, member ID, claim or authorization reference, the service and the provider; say clearly that you are requesting an internal appeal of the prior authorization denial; respond point by point to the denial reason; list the enclosures; and ask for a written decision. Under the Department of Labor rules, the appeal must be reviewed by someone who wasn't involved in the first decision, in consultation with a qualified medical professional when medical judgment is involved.

Step 4: If the appeal is denied, ask for external review

For plans that aren't grandfathered, an independent reviewer can look at the case. HealthCare.gov says you must file within 4 months of the final denial; standard reviews are decided within 45 days and expedited ones within 72 hours, and the insurer must accept the reviewer's decision. Our full guide to appealing a denied claim covers external review in more detail.

Should you get the care anyway?

That's a medical and financial decision for you and your doctor. If you go ahead before the appeal is decided, ask the provider for a cost estimate in writing and keep appealing — a later approval can still change what you owe. If you're uninsured or self-pay, you're entitled to a good faith estimate for scheduled care.

Free help

HealthCare.gov notes that your state's Consumer Assistance Program can file an appeal for you. For job-based plans, the Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration has benefits advisors at 1-866-444-3272.

A simple checklist

  • Ask your doctor's office whether the request can be corrected and resubmitted.
  • Write down the appeal deadline from your denial notice.
  • If waiting could seriously harm your health, ask your doctor to tell the plan it's urgent.
  • Request the plan's criteria and your claim file.
  • Get a letter of medical necessity from your doctor.
  • Send a clear appeal with enclosures; keep copies and proof of sending.
  • If denied again, request external review within 4 months.
