Hospital charity care and financial assistance, explained

Updated November 4, 2026 · Facts checked against the official sources listed at the end.

Many people pay hospital bills they could have had reduced or erased, simply because nobody told them to ask. Nonprofit hospitals in the U.S. are required to have a written financial assistance policy, often called charity care. Here is what that means and how to use it.

What “financial assistance” means

Financial assistance is free or discounted care that a hospital gives to patients who qualify, usually based on income and household size. CMS's patient guidance puts it simply: nonprofit hospitals must give financial assistance to eligible patients who can't afford to pay. You may also see it called “charity care.” If you got care somewhere other than a nonprofit hospital, CMS notes that the facility may still offer help, so it is always worth asking.

The federal rules behind it

To keep their tax-exempt status, nonprofit hospitals must meet requirements in Section 501(r) of the Internal Revenue Code. The IRS explains them on its website. The parts that matter most to patients:

There must be a written policy (Section 501(r)(4))

Each hospital facility must have a written financial assistance policy (FAP) that applies to all emergency and other medically necessary care. The policy must spell out:

  • who is eligible, and whether help means free or discounted care;
  • how the amount you're charged is calculated;
  • how to apply; and
  • which doctors and other providers working in the hospital are covered by the policy and which are not.

It must be easy to find

The hospital must post the policy, the application form and a plain-language summary on a website, where anyone can view and print them free without creating an account or giving personal information. Paper copies must be available free on request by mail and in public places in the hospital, including the emergency room and admissions areas. Billing statements must carry a conspicuous notice about financial assistance with a phone number and the web address for the documents. In communities with significant populations that have limited English proficiency, the documents must be translated.

Eligible patients can't be charged full price

Once a hospital decides you qualify, you may not be charged more than the “amounts generally billed” (AGB) to people who have insurance for emergency or other medically necessary care. In other words, no one who qualifies should be billed the hospital's full list price for that care.

Hospitals must give you time before aggressive collection (Section 501(r)(6))

The IRS rules create two windows that both start on the date the hospital gives you the first billing statement after you leave the hospital:

  • A 120-day notification period. The hospital must tell you about the policy and generally can't start “extraordinary collection actions” until at least 120 days after that first bill.
  • A 240-day application period. If you submit an application within 240 days, the hospital must process it. If it is incomplete, the hospital must tell you how to complete it and give you a reasonable chance to do so.

The hospital must also give you written notice at least 30 days before starting extraordinary collection actions, and the IRS notes that a hospital may continue to accept and process applications at any time. Extraordinary collection actions include selling your debt to another party, reporting you to credit bureaus, delaying or denying necessary care because of past unpaid bills, and legal actions such as liens, lawsuits, and wage garnishment.

Applying can pause collections and trigger refunds

When you turn in a complete application during the application period, the hospital must suspend any extraordinary collection actions while it decides. If you qualify, the hospital must refund anything you paid above what you owe as an eligible patient (unless it is less than $5), and take reasonably available steps to reverse collection actions, including removing adverse information it reported to credit bureaus.

These IRS rules apply to tax-exempt (nonprofit) hospital organizations. For-profit and government hospitals may have their own policies, and some states have additional charity-care laws. Ask any hospital about its policy.

How to apply, step by step

  1. Find the policy. CMS suggests searching the hospital's name plus “financial assistance,” calling the billing office, or asking for a copy if you're at the hospital.
  2. Read it before you apply. CMS recommends knowing the eligibility requirements, how to apply, the deadline, and who to call with questions.
  3. Gather documents. Usually proof of income and household size. The IRS rules say assistance can't be denied for leaving out information the policy or application form didn't specifically require.
  4. Apply in writing and keep copies. Follow the hospital's instructions. Note the date you sent it. Our free letter tool creates a cover letter that requests the policy and application and asks the hospital to pause collection while you apply.
  5. Follow up. CMS suggests asking how long processing takes, who can answer questions, and what happens to your bill in the meantime.
  6. If your bill is already with a collector, tell the collector you're applying for financial assistance and ask them to pause collections while the process plays out (CMS).

Common questions

Do I have to be uninsured?

Not necessarily. Many policies cover insured patients facing large deductibles or coinsurance. Check the eligibility section of the hospital's policy.

Can I apply after I've already paid something?

Yes, and if you qualify within the application period, the hospital must refund what you overpaid, as described above.

What if I don't qualify?

CMS suggests asking the hospital for a lower bill or a payment plan, and reaching out to a patient advocate. Also check the bill itself for errors: see how to request an itemized bill and find errors.

Checklist

  • Ask every hospital that bills you for its financial assistance policy.
  • Note the date of your first bill after discharge: the 240-day application window starts there.
  • Submit a complete application in writing; keep copies and proof of sending.
  • Tell any collector you've applied and ask them to pause.
  • If approved, check that the account is reduced and any overpayment refunded.
